For you 8 | Unique 7

Other cancer

ESMO presidential session II: divarasib beats current KRAS G12C drugs in lung cancer, and ivonescimab beats durvalumab in biliary cancer

For you 7 | Unique 7

  • KRASCENDO-1 (phase 3, n=338): divarasib vs sotorasib or adagrasib in previously treated KRAS G12C lung cancer, PFS 9.8 vs 5.7 months (HR 0.58), OS 17.7 vs 12.0 months (HR 0.56), response rate 58% vs 32%.
  • HARMONi-GI1 (phase 3, n=682): ivonescimab, a PD-1 x VEGF bispecific antibody, plus chemo vs durvalumab plus chemo in advanced biliary cancer, OS 14.9 vs 12.1 months (HR 0.75), PFS HR 0.61.
  • SAFFRON: osimertinib plus savolitinib after osimertinib failure in MET-driven lung cancer, PFS HR 0.55 and OS HR 0.68.
  • CLARITY-Gastric 01: the CLDN18.2 antibody-drug conjugate sonesitatug vedotin doubled OS in third-line gastric cancer (10.0 vs 5.0 months), though PFS missed its target.

Need to know: Second-generation targeted drugs and bispecific antibodies are beating the current standards head to head. Ivonescimab's VEGF-blocking design is one to keep an eye on, since VEGF matters in GBM too.

The FDA approved a lung cancer pill in July 2025, but US patients couldn't buy it until this week

For you 6 | Unique 6

AI updates

Anthropic launches OSS Scanner: free AI security audits for critical open-source projects, sent straight to maintainers

For you 8 | Unique 8

  • Projects opt in with a pull request; scans run offline in an isolated VM using Anthropic's strongest models, including Claude Mythos, at no cost to the project.
  • Anthropic's models flagged over 29,000 candidate vulnerabilities in 6 months, but humans could triage only about 6,000, so reports now skip human review and go straight to maintainers with a reproducer and a candidate patch.
  • In Anthropic's own audit of 97 findings, 88% met its disclosure bar and 1 was a false positive; in the first 11 days it reported 77 critical and 803 high findings, and curl shipped a fix for a use-after-free bug.
  • Trade-offs: 11 of the 97 were duplicates, and maintainers say severity can be inflated.

Need to know: This follows last week's flood of AI-found bugs. AI is now finding security holes faster than people can review them, so labs are handing the reports to the projects directly.

For you 7 | Unique 7

Lancet Digital Health: doctors ignoring correct AI hurt brain-bleed diagnosis more than doctors following wrong AI

For you 7 | Unique 7

  • 50 radiologists and 75 other physicians and trainees read head CT scans for bleeding, alone, with a plain AI verdict, and with an AI verdict that marked where it saw the bleed; one in five AI answers was deliberately wrong.
  • Some doctors followed wrong AI, but more errors came from doctors overriding correct AI, usually on cases where they felt least sure.
  • When the AI showed its evidence on the image, the odds of a correct diagnosis were 84% higher than reading alone; on the hardest cases, non-radiologists with marked-up AI beat radiologists.
  • Explanations didn't fully protect against bad AI advice.

Need to know: AI that shows its evidence on the image helps more than a bare yes/no. Relevant to how brain-imaging AI is likely to be used.

Keto updates

Nick Norwitz on a Nature Metabolism study: an ultra-processed meal gave the same glucose but a much bigger insulin response

For you 8 | Unique 7

  • The study compared two meals matched for calories, carbs, fat and protein; one was ultra-processed. It measured metabolic and brain responses.
  • Blood sugar responses were similar, but the ultra-processed meal triggered a significantly larger insulin response.
  • Norwitz's takeaways: how carbs are processed matters, blood sugar doesn't tell the whole story, and 'eat less processed food' isn't a mechanism.
  • He also questions the study's food categories; his Substack deep dive covers ways to lower the insulin response to a meal.

Need to know: This fits the glucose-plus-insulin view behind tracking GKI: a normal glucose reading can hide a big insulin spike.

General news

EPIC study: a bigger waist roughly doubles 10-year death risk in men, even at a normal BMI

For you 7 | Unique 6

  • The study followed 359,387 adults in 9 European countries for almost 10 years; 14,723 died.
  • At the same BMI, men in the largest fifth of waist size had about twice the risk of death of the smallest fifth; women about 1.8 times.
  • Each extra 2 inches (5 cm) of waist at a given BMI went with about 17% higher risk in men and 13% in women, and the pattern held at normal BMI.

Need to know: Waist size adds information BMI misses, which makes it a cheap number to track alongside body weight.
